The construction described earlier in this chapter is small enough to be shown in full, and showing it makes the tamper-evidence claim concrete rather than asserted. The example below was not composed for this report: it is produced by the project's own batching code, run over the Proof-of-Repair leaves held in the registry at artifacts/por_registry.sqlite3, and it can be regenerated with scripts/merkle_worked_example.py.

Each leaf is the SHA-256 digest of a canonical encoding of the committed fields, serialised with sorted keys and no optional whitespace and prefixed by a single zero byte [31]. Internal nodes prefix a one byte to the concatenation of their two children, so that a leaf digest can never be presented as an internal node. Where a level contains an odd number of nodes, the final node is paired with itself; the implementation applies this rule consistently and the verification path reproduces it.

Table 12 lists the four leaves recorded in the registry, in the order in which they were sealed. Three belong to the same Honda Civic and one to a Toyota Camry. The three Civic entries were sealed in separate runs during interactive testing and commit different report digests and evidence identifiers, which is why they yield three distinct leaves rather than one: a leaf commits to a sealing event, not to a repair description.

Batching these four leaves in registry order produces the tree in Figure 7. Each digest is truncated to eight hexadecimal characters for display; the values themselves are the full thirty-two byte digests.

The root of the batch is 2c5aaf4162559d21421b4f31fd06049ff98e9eb59be52b869ef0efb90e760184, a single thirty-two byte value committing to all four records. A membership proof for the fourth leaf consists of two sibling digests only, 5ba113c049c602dd and 54c97cacf508904e, sixty-four bytes in total: the verifier hashes the leaf with the first sibling on its left, hashes the result with the second sibling on its left, and compares the outcome with the anchored root. No other record in the batch is disclosed, and because proof length grows with the logarithm of batch size, a batch of one thousand repairs would still require only ten sibling digests.

## C. Case study — part 1: the fraud and why existing defences fail  
*449 words*

Odometer tampering is the clearest instance of the information asymmetry this project addresses, and it is widening rather than closing. A segment broadcast by NBC Nightly News on 1 April 2023 reported rising odometer fraud at a time when a record number of American buyers had turned to the used market [46]. The figures published since have confirmed that trend rather than contradicted it. CARFAX estimated in December 2025 that roughly 2.45 million vehicles on United States roads carry rolled-back odometers, a rise of fourteen per cent in a single year following a four per cent rise the year before, with an average loss of about 3,300 dollars for each affected buyer [47]. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration places the number of vehicles sold annually with false odometer readings near 450,000, at a cost to buyers exceeding one billion dollars a year [48].

The mechanism of the fraud has also changed in a way that matters for system design. A mechanical odometer had to be physically dismantled before it could be altered, whereas a digital instrument cluster can be rewritten with inexpensive tools that are sold openly, which is the explanation CARFAX itself offers for the increase [47]. Tampering is therefore cheap, quick, and leaves no mark on the instrument it falsifies. Detection cannot come from the vehicle, and must instead come from records held outside it.

The defences normally offered to a buyer are weaker than they first appear. Inspecting pedal wear or commissioning an independent mechanic will reveal only gross inconsistency between apparent condition and declared mileage. Checking a commercial history report for mileage discrepancies is more systematic, but it depends on records that are themselves unsigned, incomplete, and supplied voluntarily by participating garages and insurers, and it asks the buyer to trust the operator of the database rather than to verify anything independently. Where a repair record is held only as a paper invoice or an unsigned PDF, nothing about the document establishes when it was created or whether it has since been edited.

This is precisely the gap the prototype is intended to close, and the fraud is a useful test of it because the falsified quantity, the odometer reading, is one of the fields the system commits. The scenario below was therefore run end to end against the implementation rather than argued in the abstract. A repair event was sealed through the normal workshop path, batched, anchored and verified; the committed odometer reading was then altered exactly as a rollback would alter it, and the verification path was run a second time to establish whether the alteration was detected, what the buyer would observe, and what the result does and does not prove.

## D. Case study — part 2: the record that was sealed and anchored  
*440 words*

The event used for the case is a repair recorded for a 2016 Honda Civic under the sample vehicle identification number 1HGBH41JXMN109186, at an odometer reading of 98,000 kilometres, with fault code P0420 reported alongside a check-engine light, a failed emissions test and a lazy rear oxygen-sensor signal. The diagnostic context was submitted through the workshop bay form and a structured repair report was produced by the TinyLlama path with the automotive adapter; the report, the supporting evidence and the model provenance were then sealed.

Sealing produced three values. The canonical repair report hashed to 44bd1f44aea82cdd, the stored evidence payload was written to the content-addressed store under the identifier d6faec167aef4751, and the Proof-of-Repair leaf committing the vehicle identification number, the vehicle description, the odometer reading, the report digest, the evidence identifier and the model provenance was 1ecf00f00b0c9856. The leaf is small and contains no personal data, yet it fixes every one of those fields.

The leaf was then batched with one further event, a Toyota Camry record whose leaf was ac512c77df8b6a8b, under the batch identifier ui-1788331032592. Hashing the pair produced the Merkle root 46f5565e9a172ecb, which was anchored on the local development chain in transaction 0x22785f1ddd0d4b77 against the contract deployed at 0x5FbDB2315678afec, and submitted in parallel to the public OpenTimestamps calendars under receipt 526ea1c4. The receipt remained pending at the time of writing, which is the normal state for a live submission until the aggregating transaction is confirmed in a Bitcoin block, and it illustrates the point made earlier that the two anchoring paths are redundant rather than duplicated.

Verification was then run over the stored record as an independent party would run it. The report digest recomputed from the stored report reproduced the committed value; the evidence identifier recomputed from the stored bytes reproduced the committed identifier; the leaf rebuilt from the canonical payload reproduced the committed leaf; and the membership proof, consisting in this batch of the single sibling ac512c77df8b6a8b, folded the leaf back to the anchored root. All four checks passed.

One qualification must be stated plainly. The on-chain check was not repeated at the time of writing, because the local development chain was not running; the anchor record in the registry retains the transaction hash from the original submission, and no stronger claim is made here than that. This is a consequence of anchoring to a local chain rather than a public testnet, a limitation already acknowledged in this chapter, and it is precisely the dependency that a public deployment would remove. The remaining four checks require nothing beyond the record, the evidence file and the published root, and can be performed by any party holding them.

## E. Case study — part 3: rollback, invoice swap, VIN reuse, and limits  
*463 words*

The rollback was then simulated against the sealed record. Reducing the committed odometer reading from 98,000 to 76,000 kilometres, and leaving every other field exactly as it was, changed the leaf digest from 1ecf00f00b0c9856 to 3ca71fb55475126d and the recomputed root from 46f5565e9a172ecb to 263fc73abe0b57aa. Sixty-three of the sixty-four hexadecimal characters of the root differed, so the mismatch does not require careful reading to notice. The membership proof issued for the original leaf also fails when it is replayed against the altered one, because the folding no longer reproduces the anchored value. A far smaller edit behaves identically: reducing the reading by a single kilometre, to 97,999, yields the entirely unrelated leaf 93e8cccc8ccf6349 and root 9fc49570d5bd3e33. The construction offers no tolerance band within which an attacker could work.

Two further scenarios were run against the same record. The first substitutes the supporting evidence rather than the committed record. The invoice and diagnostic payload stored for this event is held under the content identifier d6faec167aef4751 and verified correctly as retrieved. Altering two bytes of that 1,966-byte file, so that the odometer figure printed on the invoice read 76,000 rather than 98,000, moved its identifier to 2c2f8a2364db9ef1. Because the leaf commits to the identifier rather than to a copy of the document, the substituted invoice no longer corresponds to anything that was anchored, and the substitution is detected without the original file being available for comparison.

The second scenario tests reuse of a genuine record across vehicles, which is the practice of presenting work carried out on one car as evidence of work carried out on another. The Camry leaf ac512c77df8b6a8b was relabelled with the Civic's vehicle identification number and description, every other committed field being left intact. The leaf digest moved to 63ab48af3bdad18c and the recomputed root to cc9b95ac3b703fb0, neither of which matches the anchored root, and the proof issued for the original leaf fails when replayed against the relabelled one. Because the vehicle identification number sits inside the hashed payload, a repair record is bound to a single vehicle and cannot be transferred to a second without the transfer becoming evident.

What the case establishes should be stated narrowly. A buyer learns that the record presented is the record that was sealed, and that it was sealed before the root was published. The buyer does not learn that the diagnosis the record contains was correct; the report sealed here labelled fault code P0420 as a cooler-circuit fault and listed coolant-system steps, which is not the accepted meaning of that code, and it was committed faithfully nonetheless. Nor does the buyer learn that every repair the vehicle received was sealed at all, since the construction cannot testify to records that were never created. Integrity and completeness are different properties, and only the first is claimed.

## F. Working of the algorithm — part 1: encoding and leaf construction  
*465 words*

The attestation layer rests on a single algorithm, applied in four stages: canonical encoding, leaf construction, tree construction, and proof. Its purpose is narrow but strict. It must produce a value that changes whenever any committed detail changes, and that two parties computing it independently from the same record will always agree upon. Determinism is therefore a requirement rather than a convenience, and each stage is specified so as to remove avoidable freedom.

Encoding is handled first. The fields to be committed are serialised as JSON with the keys sorted and all optional whitespace removed, so that a record cannot be re-serialised into a different byte string carrying the same meaning. Without this rule, two representations differing only in field order or spacing would hash to different values, and an honest verifier recomputing the digest from a faithfully stored record would obtain a mismatch indistinguishable from tampering. Canonicalisation makes the encoding a function of the content alone.

The two bulky components of a repair are reduced before the leaf is formed. The repair report is replaced by its digest, computed over the same canonical encoding, and the supporting evidence, which may include invoices, photographs and diagnostic exports, is replaced by a content identifier computed over the stored bytes [25]. The leaf therefore commits to the diagnosis and to each evidence item by reference. It remains small enough to batch cheaply and contains no personal data, while any edit to an underlying document changes the digest or the identifier that the leaf carries, and so changes the leaf.

The leaf itself is the SHA-256 digest of the canonical payload prefixed with a single zero byte [31]. The payload binds the fields listed earlier in this chapter: the vehicle identification number, the vehicle description, the odometer reading, the event type, the issuing workshop, the sealing timestamp, the report digest, the evidence identifier and the model provenance. Binding them within one digest is what prevents a record from being decomposed and recombined, since a genuine report cannot be moved to a different vehicle identification number or a different odometer reading without producing a different leaf.

Internal nodes are computed differently, as the hash of a one byte prefixed to the concatenation of the two child digests. The two prefixes separate the domains of the construction, so that a leaf digest can never be presented as an internal node, nor an internal node as a leaf. This is the standard defence against second-preimage attacks on Merkle trees, in which an attacker who can blur the distinction between a leaf and an internal node may exhibit a different set of records yielding the same root. The cost of the defence is one byte per hash; the benefit is that the structure of the tree is committed along with its contents.

## G. Working of the algorithm — part 2: tree, root, proof and verification  
*472 words*

Tree construction is iterative and operates only on digests. The leaves are taken in the order in which they were sealed and hashed in pairs to form the level above; that level is paired in turn, and the process repeats until a single value remains. Where a level holds an odd number of nodes, the final node is paired with itself, a rule the implementation applies consistently and which the verification path reproduces exactly, since a verifier that handled the odd case differently would compute a different root from identical data.

The remaining value is the Merkle root, a fixed thirty-two bytes regardless of whether the batch contains four repairs or four thousand. It is the only value that leaves the system. It is written to the smart contract together with the batch identifier and a metadata reference, and the same root is submitted to the OpenTimestamps calendars. Publication is what gives the root its force: once recorded, it fixes the contents of the batch at a point in time, and any later divergence from it becomes meaningful rather than merely surprising.

A membership proof is extracted by walking from a leaf towards the root and recording the sibling of each node visited. The proof is that list of siblings and nothing more; no other record in the batch is disclosed, which allows a single repair to be shown to a prospective buyer without revealing the other repairs committed alongside it. Proof length grows with the logarithm of batch size, so four repairs require two sibling digests and one thousand repairs require ten, which is sixty-four and three hundred and twenty bytes respectively. Verification cost grows at the same rate.

Verification inverts the construction and requires no privileged access. The verifier recomputes the report digest from the report presented, recomputes the evidence identifier from the stored bytes, rebuilds the leaf from the canonical payload, and then folds the leaf together with each sibling in turn, combining on the left or on the right according to the parity of the index at that level, until a root is produced. That root is compared with the one recorded on chain.

Agreement establishes that the record presented is the record that was committed, and that it was committed no later than the anchoring transaction. Disagreement localises the failure, because a single altered field changes its leaf, then every internal node on the path above it, and therefore the root. The avalanche behaviour of the hash function ensures that the altered root bears no resemblance to the anchored one, so the comparison is exact and requires no threshold or tolerance. What the algorithm cannot do follows from the same reasoning: it binds whatever was sealed, and it is silent on whether the diagnosis sealed was correct or whether any repair was omitted from the batch altogether.
