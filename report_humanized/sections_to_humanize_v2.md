## 1. Introduction  (replaces 1. Introduction + 1.1 Background + 1.2 Motivation + 1.3 Scope)

Used vehicles change hands on the strength of documents that cannot be checked. A buyer is handed a folder of invoices, a stamped service book and a printed job card, and asked to accept that the work described was performed, on this vehicle, at the mileage shown. None of those documents carries evidence of when it was created or whether it has been altered since, and the records that would contradict them can simply be withheld. The resulting asymmetry is the condition Akerlof described for markets in goods of uncertain quality [1], and it remains unresolved in practice [2].

Odometer tampering shows what it costs. CARFAX estimated in December 2025 that 2.45 million vehicles on United States roads carry rolled-back odometers, a rise of fourteen per cent in one year, with affected buyers losing about 3,300 dollars each [47]. The national regulator puts the number of vehicles sold annually with false readings near 450,000 [48]. Digital instrument clusters can be rewritten with inexpensive tools, so the tampering leaves no trace on the instrument it falsifies [47]. Detection must therefore come from records held outside the vehicle, and those records are precisely what cannot currently be trusted.

Two capabilities are needed, and conflating them is the common error. The first is interpretation: turning diagnostic trouble codes, symptoms and sensor observations into a structured statement of what is wrong and what must be done [29], [30]. The second is integrity: ensuring that once a repair has been sealed, neither the garage that produced it nor anyone handling it later can change the record or its supporting evidence unnoticed. The first is a language problem, addressed here with a model adapted by low-rank adaptation [10]. The second is a cryptographic problem, addressed with content-addressed evidence, Merkle commitments [20] and public anchoring [23], [35].

This paper describes a prototype that joins them, and contributes four things. A canonical Proof-of-Repair leaf binds a vehicle identification number, odometer reading, report digest, evidence identifier and model provenance within one hash. A batching path commits those leaves to an EVM contract and, independently, to a public timestamping service. A diagnostic layer adapted to automotive fault codes is evaluated on real OBD cases, including the conditions under which it fails. Finally, a demonstration run against the implementation shows that an odometer rollback, an invoice substitution and the reuse of a record across vehicles are each detected.

The separation of roles holds throughout. The model produces records and offers no guarantee that they are correct. The cryptographic layer guarantees that records cannot be altered after sealing and offers no opinion on whether they were right. What the system delivers is a repair history a stranger can verify, not a diagnosis a stranger should believe.


## 2. Related Work  (replaces 2.1 and all of 2.1.1-2.1.9, plus 2.2 Research Gap)

Four bodies of work meet in this problem, and none of them spans it.

The economic case is settled. Akerlof showed that where buyers cannot distinguish quality, price converges toward the average and honest sellers withdraw [1], and the argument has since been applied to vehicle markets and trust-free transaction systems [2]. What this literature does not supply is a mechanism for producing evidence a buyer can actually check.

Vehicle diagnosis supplies the input vocabulary. Diagnostic services and trouble codes are standardized [29], [30], as are vehicle identification numbers [28], which gives any system a stable key and a shared fault language. Data-driven work extends this toward prognosis through run-to-failure datasets and remaining-life estimation [18], [19], with broader surveys of machinery health and automotive predictive maintenance [7], [8]. That work ends at the inference; what becomes of the inference afterwards is out of its scope.

Language models supply the interpretation. Transformer architectures convert unstructured technical description into structured output [13], [14], [15], and instruction tuning improves adherence to a requested form [16]. Reliability is the known weakness, since fluent and incorrect output is a documented failure mode [9], which is why any system that commits model output must treat the commitment and the correctness as separate claims. Adaptation is now affordable: adapter layers [11], low-rank adaptation [10] and quantized adaptation [12] train a small set of parameters against a frozen backbone, and open-weight models of modest size [26], [27] put this within reach of ordinary hardware.

Integrity supplies the remaining component. Public ledgers provide append-only state with no single controller [23], programmable chains carry that further, to arbitrary state transitions defined in code [24], and vehicular proposals apply both to telemetry, forensics and ownership [3], [4], [6], [22]. Merkle trees summarise many records in one hash while preserving membership proofs [20], [31], the construction underlying certificate transparency [32], [33] and supply-chain transparency work [34]. Timestamping establishes that a document existed at a point in time without trusting the timestamper [5], [35], and content addressing allows evidence to remain off-chain while staying tamper-evident [25].

The gap is the join rather than any single component. Diagnostic systems produce text and leave its custody unaddressed. Vehicular ledgers anchor telemetry or ownership rather than repair events and the documents supporting them. Transparency logs assume a committed object whose structure and meaning are already agreed. No reviewed system binds a diagnosis, the repair performed in response to it, the evidence of that repair, and the vehicle it belongs to into one commitment a stranger can verify. Defining that committed object is therefore the first design problem, and it precedes the anchoring mechanism in what follows.


## 6. Limitations and Future Work  (replaces the current 4.5 and its ten bullets)

The limits of the prototype are stated as they stand. Anchoring runs on a local development chain, so roots carry no public persistence, and the independent timestamps remain pending until Bitcoin inclusion confirms them. The evidence is synthetic: no commercial workshop invoice has been ingested and no workshop partnership exists. Diagnostic context is typed in rather than read from a scan tool, so nothing the prototype records is taken directly from the vehicle. The evaluation rests on 57 real OBD cases, enough to show feasibility and far too few to establish how the models behave across different makes, production years and classes of fault, and the models fail outright on free-text complaints. The registry is a single SQLite file beside a local evidence directory, which suits one writer rather than concurrent multi-tenant operation. Workshop identity is unverified, and because anchoring is permissionless the submitting address records a claim rather than an authorisation. A vehicle identification number is indirectly linked to an owner, so a deployed service would need a retention and disclosure policy even though committed leaves carry no personal data.

The future work follows from each of these. The immediate steps are bundling the trained adapters with the repository, so that a clone can perform inference without manual retrieval, and publishing the contract on a public test network, which would let any party confirm a root without access to the development machine. Beyond that lie a broader evaluation corpus, grounding diagnoses in retrieved service documentation [17], reading diagnostic context directly from OBD-II hardware, and adding workshop identity with role-based access control before the system is offered to real users rather than exercised in a demonstration.


## 7. Conclusion  (new section; the paper currently has none)

Repair history is worth little if it cannot be checked. This paper described a prototype that makes it checkable by holding apart two things that are usually run together. A language model adapted by low-rank adaptation interprets diagnostic context and produces a structured repair record. A cryptographic layer binds that record to one vehicle and makes any later alteration evident. Each sealed repair becomes a canonical leaf over the vehicle identification number, the odometer reading, the report digest, the evidence identifier and the model provenance, and those leaves are gathered into a Merkle tree whose root is then anchored on an EVM contract and timestamped independently.

Measured on real OBD cases, the diagnostic layer identified the correct fault code in 98.3% of cases, and lost that accuracy entirely on free-text complaints it had not been adapted for. The attestation layer detected every alteration attempted against sealed records, including a one-kilometre odometer change, a two-byte invoice edit and the reuse of a record across vehicles. The guarantee on offer is deliberately narrow: that a record is the one that was sealed, not that the diagnosis it contains is right.


## 5.3 Case Study, closing scenarios  (replaces the invoice-substitution and record-reuse paragraphs)

Two further scenarios were run against the same record. Substituting the stored evidence was detected without reference to the original file: altering two bytes of the 1,966-byte invoice payload, so that the printed odometer figure read 76,000, moved its content identifier from d6faec167aef4751 to 2c2f8a2364db9ef1, which corresponds to nothing that was anchored. Reusing a genuine record across vehicles was equally visible: relabelling the Camry leaf ac512c77df8b6a8b with the Civic identification number, every other field left intact, moved the leaf digest to 63ab48af3bdad18c and the recomputed root to cc9b95ac3b703fb0, and the proof issued for the original leaf failed on replay. Because the vehicle identification number sits inside the hashed payload, a record cannot be moved between vehicles without the move becoming evident.
